# Module 04 — Hook and tension: why the reader keeps going

Two separate problems, often confused:

- **The hook** buys the first ten pages.
- **Narrative drive** buys the next four hundred.

A book can have a brilliant hook and no drive. Readers describe this as "it
started well." A book with drive and a weak hook is often never opened.

---

## Part 1 — The hook

### The mechanism

A hook is an **unanswered question the reader wants answered**, attached to a
person they have a reason to care about. Both halves are required. A mystery
about no one is a puzzle; a person with no question is a portrait.

### What to open with

Open at the moment the protagonist's ordinary life is under strain, shortly
before it breaks. Not in the calm before, not in the aftermath.

Working opening strategies:

1. **A person doing something specific and odd.** Curiosity about behaviour is
   the cheapest, most reliable hook.
2. **A line of dialogue mid-conflict.** The reader assembles the situation while
   already invested in the argument.
3. **A statement that creates an immediate contract.** "The day they buried my
   sister, I stole her car." Now the reader needs the next sentence.
4. **A voice worth listening to.** For literary and comic fiction, the narrator's
   way of seeing *is* the hook.
5. **A concrete threat with a clock.** For thrillers.

### What not to open with

- Weather, landscape, or the room, before anyone is in it
- A character waking up, travelling, or preparing for the day
- A prologue delivering world history
- A dream, unless the fact that it is a dream is the point and comes fast
- A funeral for someone the reader has not met
- Backstory of any length

These fail for the same reason: they ask for investment before offering a
question.

### The first-page test

By the bottom of page one, the reader should be able to answer:

- **Who** is this? (a name and one specific quality)
- **Where and when** am I? (enough to picture it, no more)
- **What is wrong?** (the trouble, present or arriving)
- **Why should I stay?** (an unanswered question, a voice, or a threat)

### The first chapter's job

- Establish the POV, tense and voice the book will keep.
- Show the protagonist's Lie operating and costing them something small.
- Raise one specific question that will not be answered for at least fifty pages.
- Deliver a scene that turns (Module 05), not a set-up.

**Promise-keeping.** The opening is a contract about the kind of book this is.
A quiet literary opening on a thriller breaks the contract, and so does a
shootout at the front of a domestic novel. Whatever tone the first five pages
establish is the tone the reader has agreed to.

## Part 2 — Narrative drive

Drive comes from **open loops**. At any moment in a well-driven novel, the
reader is holding several unanswered questions of different sizes.

### The three-layer question stack

Maintain all three, always:

| Layer | Lifespan | Example |
|-------|----------|---------|
| **Scene question** | 1–5 pages | Will she get the file before he comes back? |
| **Act question** | 50–150 pages | Is the brother involved? |
| **Book question** | whole book | Will she ever forgive herself? |

Rule: **never close two layers at once** except at the climax. When you answer a
scene question, open a new one in the same paragraph.

### The engines of tension

**1. Desire plus obstacle.** The base unit. Someone wants something; something
is in the way. No desire, no tension, regardless of how much is happening.

**2. Stakes.** What is lost if they fail. Stakes work only when they are
*specific* and *personal*. "The world ends" is weaker than "her daughter finds
out." Escalate the personal alongside the external. Stakes are conditional: as
long as nothing has actually been taken, the reader is holding a threat, not a
wound. See engine 8.

**3. Dramatic irony.** The reader knows something a character does not. This is
the single most efficient tension generator available and is badly underused.
Hitchcock's principle: a bomb going off is surprise (two seconds of shock);
a bomb the reader knows about under a table where two people chat about nothing
is suspense (ten minutes of tension).

**4. The clock.** A deadline converts vague pressure into countable pressure.
Introduce one at the midpoint if the middle is sagging.

**5. Withheld information.** Delay only what a character would plausibly not say
or not know. Artificial withholding — a POV character who conspicuously avoids
thinking about the thing they know — reads as cheating and it destroys trust.

**6. Anticipation.** Tell the reader what is coming and make them wait for it.
"She had eleven hours before the meeting she had spent three years avoiding."

**7. Contradiction.** A character behaving inconsistently with what we know
generates a question about them, which is a form of tension.

**8. Price.** Stakes are what may be lost; the **price** is what has already
been taken and does not come back. Tension needs both, and a book with only
stakes decays: if the protagonist keeps surviving intact, the reader learns
that the threats are decorative and stops paying for them. Take something
irreversibly, early, so every later threat is credible.

Three rules:

- **Paid by the protagonist.** A dead minor character costs the *reader*
  something. It is a price only where she loses.
- **Irreversible on the page.** If it can be undone, the reader is right not
  to feel it. Undoing a price once teaches them to wait out the next one.
- **In a category she values.** Taking something she is indifferent to is an
  event, not a price. The ten categories, and how to pick one, are in
  Module 03 §5b; whether the mechanic that exacts it holds up is Module 03 §5a.

### Try/fail cycles

The middle of a novel is built from attempts that do not work. Two rules:

- **Fail forward.** Each failure must produce new information or a new problem.
  Repeating the same failure is a treadmill.
- **Vary the failure mode.** Alternate `no, and` (fails, and now it is worse)
  with `yes, but` (succeeds, and the success creates a problem). Three `no, and`
  outcomes in a row reads as punishment; three `yes, but` reads as easy.

Classic pattern: try / fail worse / try harder / fail catastrophically / the
lowest point / a new understanding / the final try.

### Pacing

Pacing is the rate at which the situation changes, not the amount of action.

Accelerate with: short sentences, short paragraphs, short chapters, dialogue
with minimal attribution, scene cuts before the resolution, present-tense
immediacy, fewer descriptive beats.

Decelerate with: longer sentences, interiority, sensory detail, sequels
(Module 05), summary of unimportant intervals.

**Both are necessary.** Sustained acceleration numbs. A reader cannot feel the
fast chapters without slow ones to measure them against. Put the quiet scene
immediately before the biggest one — the contrast is what makes it land.

### Chapter endings

The end of a chapter is a decision point for the reader. Options, in rough order
of force:

1. A new threat arrives
2. A revelation that reframes what came before
3. A decision made, consequences unseen
4. A question asked and not answered
5. An image that resonates and unsettles

Do not end every chapter on a cliffhanger. Overused, it stops working and starts
reading as manipulation. Alternate hard hooks with resonant closes.

### The sagging middle — diagnosis

If the middle is slow, exactly one of these is true:

- The protagonist has become reactive. Give them a plan.
- There is no clock. Add one.
- The stakes stopped escalating. Raise them, or exact a price — take something
  that does not come back.
- The failures are repeating rather than compounding. Change the failure mode.
- The antagonist is off-stage. Bring their pressure onto the page.
- Two subplots are doing the same thematic work. Cut one.

## Hook and tension audit

- [ ] Does page one raise a question and give a reason to care about a person?
- [ ] Is there a question on the table right now that will not be answered for
      fifty pages?
- [ ] Open a page at random: what does the POV character want on this page?
      If there is no answer, the page is dead.
- [ ] Where does the reader know more than the protagonist? If nowhere, add it.
- [ ] Does every chapter end on an open state?
- [ ] Are there three consecutive scenes with the same outcome type?
- [ ] Has the protagonist lost something irreversible before the midpoint?
- [ ] Is there a slow scene immediately before the climax?

# Module 02 — Narration: ways to tell a story

Structure decides *what* happens. Narration decides *how the reader receives it*.
The same events told from a different distance are a different book.

Four decisions, made before drafting, then locked:

1. **Person** — who speaks
2. **Distance** — how close to that consciousness
3. **Tense** — when it is being told
4. **Order** — the sequence the reader receives events in

---

## 1. Person

### First person ("I")

Strongest for: voice, intimacy, unreliability, comedy, obsessive narrators.
Costs: the reader can only know what the narrator knows; the narrator's survival
is implied by the fact that they are telling this; describing the narrator is
awkward.

Requirement: a first-person narrator must have a **speech personality**. If the
narration reads like neutral prose with "I" substituted for "she," first person
is buying nothing and paying full price.

### Third person limited ("she")

The default for modern commercial and much literary fiction. One consciousness
at a time, in that character's idiom, with access to their thoughts and nothing
else.

Rule: one POV character per scene. Switch only at a scene or chapter break, and
signal the new POV within the first sentence by naming them.

### Third person omniscient

A narrator who is a distinct entity, able to move between minds and comment.
Powerful for epic scope, irony, and historical sweep. Requires a narrator with
an actual personality and consistent rules about what it will and will not tell.

Omniscient is not permission to head-hop. **Head-hopping** is drifting between
characters' interiors within a scene without a governing narrative voice. It
reads as a mistake because it usually is one.

### Second person ("you")

A specialised tool: implication, dissociation, instruction, dread. Sustainable
over a novel only when the "you" is motivated by the story itself.

## 2. Psychic distance

The most under-used control in fiction. Gardner's insight: the narrator can
stand anywhere on a continuum from satellite view to inside the skull, and the
writer moves along it deliberately.

**Level 1 — far.**
> It was the winter of 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway.

**Level 2 — identified.**
> Henry Carter stepped out into the cold and thought about the money.

**Level 3 — named interior.**
> She felt afraid.

**Level 4 — rendered interior.**
> The room was ugly. Aggressively, deliberately ugly, as if someone had made a
> project of it.

**Level 5 — fused (free indirect discourse).**
> God, the *carpet*. Who chooses a carpet like that. Who lives like this.

### How to use it

- **Open scenes wider, close in as pressure rises.** Start at 2, be at 4–5 by
  the scene's turning point.
- **Pull back for time compression, violence at a remove, or irony.**
- **Never drift.** Unintentional movement between levels is the "camera problem":
  the prose wants intimacy but keeps narrating the character from outside.

### Closing the distance — four mechanics

1. **Free indirect discourse.** Character's thoughts in the narrator's grammar,
   without quotation marks or "she thought."
2. **Delete filter verbs.** "The door was open" is closer than "She saw that the
   door was open." Filters: *saw, heard, felt, noticed, realised, watched,
   seemed, wondered*. In close third, cut them almost entirely.
3. **Selective perception.** Show only what this character would notice. A
   surgeon and a thief walk into the same room and see two different rooms.
4. **Idiom infiltration.** Let their vocabulary and judgements colour the
   narration.

### Emotion at close distance

Do not name the emotion. Naming it is level 3 in a level 5 scene.

- Weak: *She felt a wave of grief.*
- Working: *She kept setting the table for four. On the fourth night she noticed
  and left the plate where it was.*

Physical clichés (pounding hearts, clenched jaws, breath she didn't know she was
holding) are naming emotions with extra steps. Prefer a specific action, a
distorted perception, or a thought that reveals the state sideways.

## 3. Tense

**Past tense** is invisible and flexible. It is the correct default and needs no
justification.

**Present tense** creates immediacy and uncertainty, and suits close first
person, thrillers, and literary interiority. It costs flexibility: shifting time
becomes clumsy, and sustained present tense over 90,000 words can flatten.

Mixed use is legitimate: present-tense frame, past-tense narrative body.

---

## 4. Order — how time is handled

### Linear
The default. Never apologise for it. A well-told linear story beats a
badly-motivated fractured one every time.

### In medias res
Open at a point of pressure, deliver the necessary past afterwards in fragments.
Rule: the reader will tolerate confusion for exactly as long as they are
interested. Buy their patience with tension first.

### Frame narrative
A story inside a story. The frame must earn its keep by changing how the inner
story is read (an unreliable teller, a listener with a stake, a reason this is
being told *now*).

### Non-linear / braided timelines
Two or more time periods interleaved. Requirements:
- Each timeline needs its own tension and its own arc.
- Cuts should happen on rhyme or on question — end timeline A on a question that
  timeline B's opening comments on.
- The convergence must reframe both. If the past strand only explains the
  present strand, it is backstory with extra formatting.

### Flashbacks
Expensive. Every flashback stops the present story. Rules:
- No flashback before the reader cares about the present situation.
- A flashback must be a *scene*, with its own goal and turn, not a summary.
- Enter and exit cleanly with a sensory or grammatical hinge; do not stack past
  perfect for pages.
- Ask first: can this be delivered as one line of dialogue instead? Usually yes.

## 5. Narrative voice

Voice is the accumulation of choices about which details to include, which words
to use for them, what to find worth commenting on, and where to stop the
sentence. It is not adjectives.

To build a distinct voice for a book:

- **Fix the vocabulary field.** A mechanic's narrator reaches for machine
  metaphors, a gardener's for growth and decay. Choose the field and stay in it.
- **Fix the sentence rhythm.** Short and clipped, or long and accumulating.
  Vary within the pattern; do not abandon it.
- **Fix what the narration *judges*.** A narrator that finds everything equally
  interesting has no personality.
- **Fix the humour register**, including "none."

Test: take three paragraphs from chapter 30, strip the character names, and see
whether they could be swapped with three paragraphs from any other book of the
same genre. If yes, the book has no voice.

---

## 6. Exposition

The hardest technical problem in narration. Rules:

1. **Withhold until the reader is asking.** Information delivered before the
   question is raised reads as homework. The same information delivered after
   reads as reward.
2. **Only what the scene needs now.** Not the whole history of the kingdom.
3. **Deliver it inside conflict.** Characters who want opposing things reveal
   context as ammunition.
4. **No "as you know" dialogue.** Two people who both know something do not
   explain it to each other.
5. **Trust the reader for the first 30 pages.** Unexplained specifics create
   curiosity; over-explained specifics create boredom. Explain later, when it
   pays a scene.

---

## Narration audit

- [ ] Is the POV character named in the first sentence of every scene?
- [ ] Does any scene contain a second character's interior? Remove it.
- [ ] Count filter verbs per 1,000 words in close-POV scenes. Above ~5, revise.
- [ ] Does psychic distance close as scene tension rises?
- [ ] Do the narrators of a multi-POV book have measurably different vocabularies?
- [ ] Is any block of exposition longer than 150 words? Break it and distribute it.
- [ ] Does any flashback appear before the reader has a reason to care?

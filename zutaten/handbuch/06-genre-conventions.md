# Module 06 — Genre conventions and reader expectations

Genre is a contract, not a shelf label. A reader who picks up a crime novel has
paid for a specific set of experiences and will feel cheated if they are not
delivered — even if the book is well written in every other respect.

The Story Grid framing is the most useful one here: every genre has
**conventions** (elements the story must contain) and **obligatory scenes**
(moments the reader is waiting for and will not forgive being skipped).

Deliver the obligatory scenes. Subvert *how* they are delivered, never *whether*.

---

## The universal method

For any genre, before outlining, answer:

1. **What emotional experience is this genre selling?** (Crime: the restoration
   of order. Horror: dread. Romance: the earned intimacy. Thriller: the escape
   from death.)
2. **What are the conventions?** — the character roles, settings, and objects
   the reader expects to be present.
3. **What are the obligatory scenes?** — the moments they will feel robbed
   without.
4. **What is the ending shape?** — genres have permitted endings. Violating this
   is the one unforgivable move.

## Genre reference

### Crime / Mystery / Detective

**Conventions:** detective, criminal, victim. A crime committed before or early
in the book. Clues available to the reader. Red herrings. A closed or
semi-closed suspect pool.

**Obligatory scenes:**
- Discovery of the crime
- The detective takes the case, personally
- Investigation with at least one wrong theory pursued seriously
- The detective at the mercy of the criminal (the moment of real danger)
- Exposure of the criminal, and the explanation

**Ending shape:** the crime is solved. The moral order may be restored or shown
to be unrestorable, but the *puzzle* is answered.

**Fair play:** every clue needed to solve it must have appeared on the page.
Withholding the decisive fact until the reveal is a breach of contract.

---

### Thriller

**Conventions:** a hero, a villain with a plan already in motion, an innocent
population at risk, a ticking clock, a chase.

**Obligatory scenes:**
- The inciting attack or discovery of the plot
- The hero realises the scope of the threat and no one believes them
- The villain demonstrates their power, on-page, to someone the reader likes
- The hero is defeated and loses everything at ~75%
- Hero and villain meet face to face at the climax

**Ending shape:** the threat is stopped, usually at a high price. Ambiguity is
permitted about the aftermath, not about the confrontation.

**Pacing:** short chapters, present pressure, and the reader ahead of the hero
often enough to generate dread.

---

### Romance

**Conventions:** two leads with real chemistry and real incompatibility. Each has
a wound making love difficult. Obstacles both external and internal. The
relationship is the plot, not a subplot.

**Obligatory beats** (Gwen Hayes' *Romancing the Beat*, condensed):

| Beat | Position | Function |
|------|----------|----------|
| Setup — each lead's wound and barrier | 0–10% | Why love is impossible for them |
| Meet | ~10% | First encounter, with charge |
| No Way | 10–20% | The reason this cannot work |
| Adhesion | ~20% | Something forces them into proximity |
| Deepening desire | 20–45% | Attraction and trust build, slowly |
| No going back | ~40% | Irreversible emotional commitment |
| Midpoint of love | 50% | Peak intimacy and vulnerability |
| Inkling of doubt | 55–65% | The wound stirs |
| Deeper doubt / retreat | 65–80% | Defences go back up |
| Dark night | ~80% | They are apart; it looks final |
| Wake-up | ~85% | Each sees their own error |
| Grand gesture | 90–97% | Vulnerability at a price, earning reconciliation |
| HEA / HFN | 97–100% | Happily Ever After, or Happy For Now |

**Ending shape:** HEA or HFN. This is non-negotiable. A romance without it is a
love story, which is a different genre with a different readership.

**Rule:** the obstacle must be emotional, not merely logistical. A couple kept
apart only by a misunderstanding that one conversation would fix is not a
romance, it is an annoyance.

### Fantasy

**Conventions:** a coherent secondary world or an intrusion into ours. A magic
system with stated **costs, limitations and weaknesses** (see "The magic's cost"
below). A power the protagonist lacks and must earn or
refuse. A scope of threat proportional to the world.

**Obligatory scenes:**
- The threshold crossing into the magical
- The rules of magic demonstrated through failure, not explained
- A **price** paid for power — not merely a **cost** spent to use it
- The final confrontation where the magic's limits determine the outcome

**Rules:**
- Magic that solves problems without cost destroys tension. See "The magic's
  cost" below.
- World-building is delivered through friction — a character wanting something
  the world makes difficult — never through a tour.
- Names and terminology: fewer, and used consistently. Every invented word
  spends reader attention.

#### The magic's cost — Sanderson's laws

Three working laws, formulated by Brandon Sanderson and now standard
vocabulary. They govern the **cost** of a system; the **price** paid by a
character is a separate question, handled in Module 03 §5b. The last
paragraph here is where the two meet.

**First law.** The reader's satisfaction with a magical solution is
proportional to how well they understand the magic. This produces the
division:

| | Rules stated? | May it resolve the climax? | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hard magic** | yes, explicitly | yes | problem-solving, plot mechanics |
| **Soft magic** | no | **no** | wonder, threat, scale |

The trap is the middle: a system explained enough to feel like rules but not
enough to be checked. Either state the rules and let them decide the ending,
or withhold them and never let magic rescue anyone. Gandalf's abilities are
never specified precisely so that he cannot be asked why he did not simply
solve it.

**Second law.** *Limitations are more interesting than powers.* What the
magic cannot do generates more story than what it can. Three distinct things
are routinely collapsed into one; keep them apart:

| | Definition | Produces | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | What is spent per use | budgeting, choice under scarcity | metal burned; body heat; a year of life |
| **Limitation** | What the system fundamentally cannot do | plots that must be solved another way | no one is brought back; it works once per person |
| **Weakness** | Where it backfires or can be turned against the user | vulnerability, antagonist leverage | the mist drives you mad; the cure is also the poison |

A system with a cost but no limitations produces an arms race. A system with
limitations produces plots.

**Third law.** Expand what exists before adding what does not. Three rules
with twenty consequences beats twenty rules. Every new mechanic must be
audited for what it now trivially solves.

**Zeroth law**, added later and half in jest: when the laws conflict, choose
what is compelling. It is not a licence to ignore the first three; it is
permission to break one of them once, on purpose, for a scene the book is
being written for.

**Where cost becomes price.** A cost is bookkeeping until it does one of two
things: forces a choice between two things she wants, or takes something in
one of the ten categories in Module 03 §5b. Design the system so those two
coincide — so the resource the magic consumes is the thing the character most
needs to keep. Kvothe's sympathy consumes body heat, but the price of his
talent is isolation; if the system's cost and the character's price run on
separate ledgers, the book has accounting where it needs a wound.

**Checks:**
- [ ] Hard or soft? Stated before the first use, and consistent afterwards.
- [ ] Named cost, named limitations, named weakness — all three, separately.
- [ ] For each new mechanic: which earlier problem does it now solve for free?
- [ ] Does the system's cost fall on what the protagonist values, or on a
      resource she can simply acquire more of?

**Cozy fantasy (sub-mode).** The conventions above are written for epic and
high fantasy. In cozy fantasy they are scaled down, not removed:

- Magic is *inconvenient*, not *punishing*. In the vocabulary above, cozy
  fantasy runs on **limitations** and **weaknesses**, not on **costs**: it
  only works on people who come willingly; it works once per person; it always
  shows the truth, including the truth she does not want. Limitations generate
  complications and decisions without damaging the protagonist, which is
  exactly what the contract needs. Where a cost is used, it is time, effort,
  standing, money, or a small personal sacrifice. Magic that permanently
  damages the protagonist's body or mind is tragic-mode and conflicts with the
  cozy contract; use it at most once in a premise pool, never as the default.
- The strongest cozy price mechanic is not consumption but **condition**: the
  magic requires an act that the protagonist's Wound forbids her. No tank, no
  scar — a recurring effort that costs her every time, and that she can refuse.
- The climax is decided *within* the magic's rules, not necessarily *by* them.
  A climax decided by a human choice that the magic's rules make costly is
  fully compliant. The obligatory scene "the magic's limits determine the
  outcome" reads, in cozy mode, as "the outcome does not violate the limits".
- Stakes are personal and communal: a business, a home, a place in a
  community, a relationship. Nothing apocalyptic. No countdown may run through
  more than half the book; cozy readers need days without a clock.
- The setting is a place the reader wants to return to, and the book returns
  to it. An ensemble of at least four recurring characters lives there, each
  with a want of their own.
- The emotional experience sold is *safety with engagement*: the reader must
  be sure things will end well and still care how. Low stakes mean every
  character interaction has to matter.

### Science fiction

**Conventions:** a novum (the one thing changed), rigorously followed
consequences, a society altered by it, a protagonist positioned to feel that
alteration.

**Obligatory scenes:**
- The novum demonstrated in operation
- Its social consequence shown through an ordinary person
- The moment the idea's price lands on the protagonist personally

**Rule:** the idea must be inseparable from the plot. If the same story could be
told without the science-fictional element, it is set dressing.

---

### Horror

**Conventions:** an unheroic, under-powered protagonist. A monster or force that
does not negotiate. A conventional setting invaded by the unnatural. Isolation.

**Obligatory scenes:**
- The first sign, dismissible
- The escalation that removes the possibility of dismissal
- The failed escape
- Confrontation with the monster, on its terms

**Ending shape:** survival at a price, or defeat. Clean victory dissolves the genre.

**Rule:** dread beats gore. What is suggested and withheld frightens; what is
fully described stops frightening.

---

### Literary fiction

See [Module 07](07-literary-mode.md). Its conventions are different in kind:
what is promised is insight, voice, and precision rather than plot resolution.

## Word count by genre

Length is part of the contract. These are current publishing expectations for
adult fiction; self-publishing tolerates wider ranges but reader expectation
still applies.

| Category | Target |
|---|---|
| Literary fiction | 80,000–95,000 |
| Commercial / upmarket fiction | 80,000–100,000 |
| Mystery / thriller | 80,000–90,000 |
| Contemporary romance | 70,000–90,000 |
| Historical romance | 80,000–100,000 |
| Science fiction | 90,000–110,000 |
| Fantasy / epic fantasy | 90,000–120,000 |
| Romantasy | 90,000–110,000 |
| Young adult | 60,000–80,000 |
| Middle grade | 40,000–60,000 |
| Novella | 20,000–50,000 |

Convert the target into a scene budget before drafting: 90,000 words at an
average scene of 1,800 words is 50 scenes. Allocate them across the structure
from Module 01.

## Tropes

A trope is a familiar pattern the reader actively wants. Enemies to lovers.
The locked room. The mentor's death. The heist crew assembled.

- **Tropes are not clichés.** A cliché is a trope executed without specificity.
- Genre readers **seek** tropes and search for them by name. Delivering them is
  service, not laziness.
- The craft is in the execution: keep the shape, replace the details, and give
  the trope a reason to exist in *this* character's arc.
- A trope that produces the same mechanism in every candidate (forced proximity
  as "he is quartered in her house", every time) has stopped being a trope and
  become a template. Vary the mechanism or drop the trope.

---

## Mixing genres

Every book has a **primary** genre that sets the ending shape, and may have a
secondary.

Rule: deliver the primary genre's obligatory scenes in full. The secondary
genre's obligations may be delivered in compressed form. A fantasy romance must
end HEA if romance is primary, and may end in bittersweet sacrifice if fantasy
is primary — but the reader must be able to tell which they bought within the
first three chapters.

## Genre audit

- [ ] Which genre is primary? Can a reader tell by page 20?
- [ ] List the obligatory scenes. Is each of them on the page, dramatized?
- [ ] Does the ending match the genre's permitted shapes?
- [ ] Is the word count inside the expected band?
- [ ] Which three tropes am I delivering, and how is each made specific?
- [ ] For mysteries: is every clue on the page before the reveal?
- [ ] For fantasy: hard or soft, stated? Are cost, limitations and weakness
      each named separately, and does the system's cost constrain the climax?
- [ ] Which of the ten price categories (Module 03 §5b) does this book use,
      and does the protagonist own something in it?
- [ ] For cozy fantasy: are the stakes personal, does the magic run on
      limitations rather than costs, is there a place the book returns to, and
      is no clock running through more than half the book?
- [ ] For romance: is the obstacle emotional rather than logistical?

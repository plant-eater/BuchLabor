# Module 01 — Structure

## What structure actually is

Structure is not a template to fill. It is a schedule of *irreversible changes*.
A book is well structured when, at regular intervals, something happens that
makes it impossible for the story to go back to how it was five pages ago.

Every model below is a different accounting of the same underlying question:
**when does the situation change so that it cannot change back?**

Pick one skeleton. Do not blend three. Blending produces a book with beats
everywhere and pressure nowhere.

---

## 1. The three-act spine (the default)

The base layer under almost every commercial novel.

| Act | Share | Job |
|-----|-------|-----|
| I | first 25% | Establish the normal, break it, force a choice |
| II | middle 50% | Escalating attempts, each failing in a new way |
| III | final 25% | Forced confrontation, resolution, new normal |

Two hinges carry the whole thing:

- **End of Act I (~25%):** the protagonist makes a *choice* that closes the
  door behind them. Not something happening to them. Something they do.
- **End of Act II (~75%):** the lowest point. The protagonist's strategy has
  been proven wrong, not merely unlucky.

The classic weak point is the middle of Act II. The fix is the midpoint (below).

---

## 2. Save the Cat — 15 beats

The most operational model available. Use it when the book is commercial and
needs to move. Percentages are of total word count.

| # | Beat | % | Function |
|---|------|---|----------|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | A snapshot of the flawed world, tuned to contrast with beat 15 |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | Someone says the thing the protagonist does not yet believe |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10 | Ordinary life; show the flaw operating and costing them |
| 4 | Catalyst | 10 | The inciting event. External, undeniable, unignorable |
| 5 | Debate | 10–20 | Refusal, hesitation, "is there another way" |
| 6 | Break Into Two | 20–25 | The active choice. New world, new rules |
| 7 | B Story | ~22 | The relationship that will carry the theme |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 20–50 | The premise delivered. The reason someone bought this book |
| 9 | **Midpoint** | 50 | False victory or false defeat; stakes raised; clock starts |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 50–75 | The opposition adapts; allies fracture; the flaw does damage |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | The worst thing. Often a literal or symbolic death |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–80 | The character sits in the wreckage and reckons |
| 13 | Break Into Three | 80 | The realisation. Usually delivered by the B story |
| 14 | Finale | 80–99 | Acts on the new truth; confronts the antagonist; the price is paid |
| 15 | Final Image | 99–100 | Mirror of beat 1, transformed |

**Use it as a schedule, not a checklist.** Convert the percentages into word
counts before drafting: at 90,000 words, the Midpoint is around word 45,000,
which is roughly chapter 22 of 45. Write that number down.

**Midpoint discipline.** The midpoint is what stops the sagging middle. It must
do at least two of: reverse the protagonist's understanding of the situation,
convert them from reactive to proactive, introduce a deadline, or reveal the
antagonist's real objective.

## 3. Dan Harmon's Story Circle — 8 steps

Best for character-driven work, series episodes, and any story that returns to
where it began. It is the Hero's Journey compressed into something checkable.

1. **Comfort zone** — a character in their routine
2. **Want** — they need something
3. **Unfamiliar situation** — they cross into an unfamiliar world
4. **Adapt** — they adapt to it, paying to learn its rules
5. **Get** — they get what they wanted
6. **Pay** — and pay a heavy price for it
7. **Return** — they come back to the familiar situation
8. **Changed** — having been fundamentally changed

The circle's strength is the symmetry of 1↔5 and 4↔8. Its diagnostic value: if
step 6 costs nothing, the story has no meaning; if step 8 shows no change, the
story had no reason to be told.

---

## 4. Seven-Point Structure

Lean, good for thrillers and for plotting backwards.

Hook → Plot Turn 1 → Pinch 1 → Midpoint → Pinch 2 → Plot Turn 2 → Resolution

Build it **from the ends inward**: write the Resolution first, then the Hook as
its opposite state, then the Midpoint as the pivot between them. Pinches are
pressure applied by the antagonist, and they are what most drafts forget.

---

## 5. Kishōtenketsu — four movements

Ki (introduction) → Shō (development) → **Ten (turn)** → Ketsu (reconciliation)

The turn (*ten*) is not a plot twist. It is the arrival of something that
reframes everything already shown — a new perspective, a parallel situation, an
unrelated element that turns out to be the point. The conclusion reconciles the
first two movements with the third.

This structure is **goal-based rather than conflict-based**. It is not
conflict-free; it simply does not organise itself around a protagonist beating
an opponent. Use it for slice-of-life, atmospheric, and literary work, for
sections inside a larger conflict-driven book, and for individual chapters where
the point is a shift in understanding rather than a shift in position.

---

## 6. The Fichtean Curve

No extended set-up. Open inside crisis, then a rising series of crises, each
higher than the last, then climax and a very short falling action.

Use when: thrillers, horror, anything short, anything where the reader has been
promised speed. Backstory is delivered in small doses between crises, never in a
block.

---

## Choosing

| Situation | Skeleton |
|-----------|----------|
| Commercial novel, needs to sell | Save the Cat |
| Character transformation is the point | Story Circle |
| Thriller, tight, plotted backwards | Seven-Point |
| Literary, atmospheric, low external stakes | Kishōtenketsu |
| Fast, relentless, short | Fichtean Curve |
| Ensemble or multi-thread epic | Three-act spine per POV thread, braided |

---

## Multi-thread structure

For books with several POV threads:

1. Give each thread its **own** complete arc with its own hinges.
2. Stagger the hinges. Two threads should not hit "All Is Lost" in the same
   chapter unless you are doing it deliberately for a convergence.
3. Threads must **converge**, not merely alternate. If thread B could be deleted
   without changing thread A's outcome, thread B is a different book.
4. Cut away from a thread on an open question, return to it when the reader's
   memory of that question is still warm — typically within two chapters.

---

## Chapters versus scenes

- A **scene** is a unit of story (Module 05).
- A **chapter** is a unit of *reading*, chosen for rhythm and stopping points.

Rules of thumb: 1–3 scenes per chapter. 1,500–3,500 words for most fiction,
800–2,000 for thrillers and page-turners. Vary the length deliberately — a
900-word chapter after three long ones reads as acceleration.

End chapters on the second-to-last beat of the scene wherever possible, so the
reader must continue to get the resolution.

## Structural audit

Answer with page numbers. Any answer of "somewhere in the middle" is a failure.

- [ ] What is the irreversible choice at 25%, and did the protagonist make it?
- [ ] Does the midpoint change the protagonist from reactive to proactive?
- [ ] At 75%, has the protagonist's *method* been disproven, not merely blocked?
- [ ] Does the climax force the protagonist to use the thing they learned?
- [ ] Is the final image in dialogue with the opening image?
- [ ] Can any chapter be deleted with no consequence downstream? Delete it.
- [ ] Does the antagonist have their own three-act structure? They should.

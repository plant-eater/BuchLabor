# Module 03 — Character

Readers do not remember plots. They remember people under pressure. A structurally
perfect book with hollow characters fails; a loosely structured book with a
person the reader believes in survives.

This module is the highest-leverage one for an AI writer, because generic
characterisation is the model's strongest default and its most damaging one.

---

## 1. The engine: five interlocking parts

Define these five for the protagonist before writing a word. They are not
biography. They are a machine that generates behaviour.

| Part | Question | Example |
|------|----------|---------|
| **Ghost** | What happened before page 1 that still runs them? | At nineteen she talked her brother into a job that killed him |
| **Wound** | What is the ongoing pain the ghost left? | She believes her judgement gets people hurt |
| **Lie** | What false belief did they adopt to survive the wound? | "If I never decide, no one dies because of me" |
| **Want** | What concrete external thing do they pursue? | To hand the investigation to someone senior and go home |
| **Need** | What must they accept to be whole? | That refusing to act is also a decision, with its own bodies |

The chain runs one direction: **Ghost → Wound → Lie → Weakness → Want.** The Need
is the Truth that dissolves the Lie.

**The Want and the Need must be in tension.** If the character can get both by
doing the same thing, there is no internal story. The plot's job is to make the
Want and the Need mutually exclusive at the climax, and force a choice.

Do this for the antagonist too. An antagonist without a coherent Lie is a plot
device.

**When this is built.** The five parts are step 3 of the order of operations.
A premise (step 1) is not expected to contain them, and a premise must not be
rejected for lacking a Want/Need exclusion, a midpoint, or a climax mechanic.
Those are the *output* of this module, not its input. What can be tested at
premise level is listed in section 5a.

## 2. Arc types

### Positive change arc (the default)
Believes the Lie → the plot punishes it → grasps the Truth → acts on it at cost.
Beats: the Lie is comfortable in Act I, expensive at the midpoint, unaffordable
at 75%, abandoned in Act III.

### Flat arc
The character already holds the Truth. The world believes the Lie. The character
does not change; they change everyone around them, at cost to themselves.
Use for: series protagonists, mentors, detectives, most action heroes.
Requirement: the Truth must be *tested* hard enough that holding it hurts.

### Disillusionment arc (negative)
Lie → Truth, but the truth is bleak. They see clearly and are damaged by clarity.
Ends jaded but awake.

### Fall arc (negative)
Lie → offered the Truth → rejects it → embraces a worse Lie. Ends in ruin.
The engine is repeated *choices*, each defensible, each worse.

### Corruption arc (negative)
Truth → tempted by a Lie → knowingly takes it. Ends morally compromised.
Hardest to write, because the reader must understand the temptation. Give the Lie
real advantages and let the character be right about something.

**Rule for all negative arcs:** the reader must see the exit the character
refuses. Tragedy without an available alternative is just misfortune.

## 3. Making a character specific

The generic character is the model's default output. Defeat it deliberately.

**Give them a competence.** Something they know in detail that the story can use.
Competence is the fastest route to reader respect.

**Give them a contradiction.** A meticulous person with one chaotic domain. A
coward who is fearless about exactly one thing. Contradiction is what makes a
character read as a person rather than a description.

**Give them an unattractive quality that costs them something.** Not a
"flaw" like "works too hard." Something the reader has to forgive.

**Give them a vocabulary.** What metaphors do they reach for? What do they
never say?

**Give them an opinion about something irrelevant.** People have positions on
things that do not matter. Characters who only have opinions about the plot are
plot functions.

**Give them a body.** How they occupy space, what they do with their hands, what
they physically avoid.

### The generic-character test
Write the character's three defining traits on a line. If those three traits fit
a stock type ("gruff mentor with a heart of gold", "sarcastic hacker",
"world-weary detective"), you have written a type. Keep one trait, replace the
other two with something drawn from the Ghost.

## 4. Motivation

Every character in every scene wants something *in that scene*, and it must be
concrete enough to be won or lost. "To be happy" is not a scene goal. "To get
out of this room without agreeing to anything" is.

Layered motivation:

- **Story goal:** what they pursue across the whole book
- **Scene goal:** what they pursue in this scene
- **Subconscious goal:** what they are actually doing, which they would deny

The gap between the second and third produces subtext.

## 5. Supporting characters

Each significant supporting character needs:

- A **want** independent of the protagonist. They existed before page 1 and had
  plans.
- A **function**: pressure, contrast, information, or theme. If a character has
  no function, merge them into another character. Merging is almost always an
  improvement.
- A **stance toward the protagonist's Lie**: reinforcing it, challenging it, or
  embodying its consequences.

The **B-story character** carries the theme. They are usually the one who says
the thing that triggers the Act III realisation. Introduce them around 20–25%.

### Antagonist
- Wants something specific and, from inside their frame, justified.
- Is the protagonist's Lie taken to its logical conclusion, or the Truth's cost
  made flesh.
- Has a plan that works, that the protagonist must actively defeat.
- Is *present* in Act II. An antagonist who only appears at the climax is a
  monster reveal, not an opposition.

### 5a. Premise-level checks for antagonist, partner and price

These six checks are the only character tests that apply *before* the
Ghost/Wound/Lie work, at the premise stage. Five were learned from a premise
round in which nineteen candidates failed, most on one of these; the sixth
follows from the price taxonomy in 5b. Each is a yes/no question; a "no" is
answered with a repair, not a rejection.

- **The antagonist's plan runs without the protagonist's failure.** An
  antagonist who has already acted before page 1, or who wins by waiting for
  a deadline, is a clock, not an opponent. He must have a next step she can
  interrupt.
- **The antagonist needs the protagonist.** If he has a working substitute
  for whatever he wants from her — a second craftsman, a fallback procedure —
  her decision does not change whether his goal happens, only who pays. Then
  there is no contest.
- **The love interest does not hold the solution.** If he can end the book by
  speaking one sentence or performing one act, every scene in which he does
  not is either indulgence or a deus ex machina in waiting. He may hold a
  piece; not the whole.
- **The price is not a currency.** If every obstacle dissolves with more
  payment (more hours, more miles, more words), the mechanic is a **cost**,
  not a price: the climax becomes a question of quantity, and giving more is
  not changing. A cost becomes a price at the point where spending it forces
  a choice between two things she wants, or alters who she is. The test:
  can she solve the book by paying harder? Then there is no price.
- **The price has no refund.** If success automatically returns what was paid
  — the burden lifts once the job is done well — nothing was risked. Check
  whether the mechanic names a condition under which the loss reverses and
  whether she would meet that condition anyway by doing her job.
- **The price is hers.** The mechanic must take from the protagonist, not from
  a bystander, a client, or a village. A price borne by a third party makes
  her decision a question of who suffers, not of what she becomes — and it
  reads as callousness rather than sacrifice. (A mechanic that deliberately
  takes from others is a valid antagonist design; it is not a protagonist
  price.)

Two further checks belong to the love line and are listed here because they
fail at premise level as often as the ones above: the partner needs a wound of
his own that only she can reach, and the obstacle between them must be a need,
not a secret — withheld information is dissolved by one conversation.

### 5b. The kinds of price

A price is chosen, not discovered. Ten categories, roughly ordered by severity.
The right one is rarely the most severe.

| # | Kind | What is taken | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Life** | Her own, given deliberately | Roy Batty; Sydney Carton |
| 2 | **Body** | A hand, an eye, health, fertility | Frodo's finger; Oedipus |
| 3 | **Relationship** | A person, by death or by renunciation | Katniss and Prim; Rick and Ilsa |
| 4 | **Moral integrity** | Something done that cannot be undone or excused | Michael Corleone; Walter White |
| 5 | **Identity** | The account of herself she was living by | K in *Blade Runner 2049* |
| 6 | **Belonging** | Home, community, a place in the world | exile, expulsion, a door closed behind her |
| 7 | **Standing** | Reputation, profession, licence, rank | the whistleblower; the struck-off doctor |
| 8 | **Capability** | The very ability she won with | Ged's power; Prospero's staff |
| 9 | **Time** | Years that do not come back | Cooper in *Interstellar* |
| 10 | **Possibility** | The life that will now never happen | Frodo cannot live in the Shire he saved |

**How to choose.**

- **Strike what she has been defending.** The price must land in the category
  the protagonist spent Act I protecting. Walter pays with the family; Ged
  pays with power. A price in a category she is indifferent to is an event.
- **She must own something there.** Check, before committing, that she
  actually possesses something in the chosen category. A character with no
  standing cannot pay with standing, and a protagonist who holds nothing in
  any of the ten has a structural problem upstream, not a price problem.
- **Categories 6, 8 and 10 keep working after the climax.** 1, 2 and 3 are
  scenes; belonging, capability and possibility are the rest of her life.
  Where the ending must resonate rather than resolve, prefer them.
- **Category 3 is the most used and the least distinguishing.** A dead mentor
  is the default of the form. It is not wrong; it is not, on its own, enough.
- **One primary, at most one secondary.** Three prices read as punishment and
  the reader stops attributing meaning to any of them.

**Genre defaults.** These are expectations, not rules; deviating is a choice
to make on purpose.

| Genre | Usual | Forbidden |
|---|---|---|
| Thriller | 1, 3, 9 | — |
| Mystery | 4, 7 | — |
| Epic fantasy | 2, 8, 1 | — |
| Science fiction | 5, 10, 6 | — |
| Horror | 1, 2, 5 | a price that is fully repaid |
| Romance | 5, 7, 10 | 3 where it is the love line itself |
| Cozy fantasy | 6, 7, 10, a small 3 | 1, 2 as the default (see Module 06) |
| Literary | 4, 5, 10 | a price that resolves the theme |

## 6. Relationships

Relationships are more interesting than individuals. Build each significant pair
with:

- **A history** — one specific shared event, referenced obliquely
- **An imbalance** — one of them needs the other more
- **An unsaid thing** — the topic they steer around
- **A change** — the relationship must be different at the end

Two characters who agree about everything produce dead pages. Give allies real
disagreements about method, not just about the plan.

---

## 7. Interiority

The distinguishing feature of prose fiction over film is access to thought. Use it.

Interiority is not "she wondered what would happen next." It is:

- **Judgement** — what they think of what they are seeing
- **Association** — what this reminds them of
- **Rationalisation** — the story they tell themselves about what they just did
- **Contradiction** — thinking one thing while doing another

The most valuable interiority is the character being **wrong about themselves**.
Readers derive enormous pleasure from seeing past a narrator's self-image.

---

## Character audit

- [ ] Can I state the Ghost, Lie, Want and Need in one sentence each?
- [ ] Are Want and Need mutually exclusive at the climax?
- [ ] Does the antagonist have their own Lie and their own justified want?
- [ ] Does the antagonist pass the five checks in section 5a?
- [ ] Does every supporting character want something the protagonist does not?
- [ ] Is there a scene where the protagonist acts *against* their own interest
      because of the Lie, and the reader can see it?
- [ ] If I swapped the protagonist's dialogue with another character's, would
      anyone notice?
- [ ] Does the protagonist make the final decisive choice themselves? (If someone
      else solves it, the arc is void.)
- [ ] Has the protagonist changed in a way that costs them something they wanted?
- [ ] Which price category (5b) is paid, is it irreversible, and is it hers?
